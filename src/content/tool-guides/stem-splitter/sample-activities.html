Prepare a shared split so students can start listening without spending a split run. For annotation, choose Class: listen and add notes and press Save sharing. Chat activities need no more than two or three Listening Guy turns per signed-in student; check the daily allowances in Settings.

1. One Stem at a Time

Use a four-stem split without introductory notes so students can make their own observations. No special teaching instruction is needed. Play the first minute with all stems, then solo Vocals and replay it. Ask for one sentence about pitch, rhythm, or phrasing, rather than only the lyrics.

Repeat with Drums, Bass, and Other, then hear bass and drums together. Have students name the instruments they think they hear in Other and check against the full mix. Each student pins a note at a surprising moment. For a variation, use six stems to add guitar and piano, or ask students to predict instruments before listening.

2. Mute the Melody

Choose a clear lead vocal. During the chorus, students mute Vocals, try to hum the melody, and identify any remaining stem carrying it. Then they hear the voice alone and consider what helps them recognize the song.

Use the texture instruction from the Instructor Console to introduce monophony and polyphony. Students ask Listening Guy whether parts carry one melodic line or independent lines, then solo the named stems and pin a note where its account holds or fails. Several instruments do not necessarily mean independent lines: ask what each part is doing.

3. Find the Form

Give each group a split in a folder that allows class notes. Students listen at full speed and mark the sections they hear: intro, verse, chorus, bridge, solo, or outro. At two boundaries, they name a stem that signals the change, such as a new drum pattern, a bass pause, or backing vocals entering.

Groups read one another's notes and add a separate note where they disagree. Use [ and ] to revisit annotations during discussion. If a boundary is unclear, solo a part that changes there and listen again.

4. Ask, Then Verify

Use any split and reserve two Listening Guy turns per student for questions and a follow-up. Students choose one claim about an instrument or section from the opening guide, solo the relevant stem, find the passage, and press L to loop it.

Ask for a three-sentence note: state the claim, explain the mixer action, then describe whether the sound supports it. Students send their evidence in a follow-up and check whether Listening Guy revises its answer. Notice when the model admits uncertainty or treats a genre convention as a fact. It cannot hear the audio, so its guide supplies no direct audio evidence.

5. Slow Rhythm Lab

Choose a distinctive groove, such as a clave, shuffle, or second-line pattern. Begin with Drums at 0.5×. Students loop four bars, count aloud, write “1 & 2 & …,” and mark accents. Add Bass and ask whether its attacks coincide with the kick drum or fall between the beats.

Return to 1× and check the pattern again. If little percussion leaves Drums nearly empty, try Other or Guitar when either contains the rhythmic part. Have students explain how the chosen stem helped.

Adapt the Format

For asynchronous work, start a discussion from classmates' notes. In a large lecture, lead One Stem at a Time or Slow Rhythm Lab as a live session, then assign Find the Form independently. A prepared recording costs students no split run to open, leaving their allowance for their own experiments.
